Saldon's Summer 2026 Work Term Report!

saldon at her desk attending a meeting while catching up on her daily intake of fruits!

From June to August 2026, I worked as a Junior Developer at Jack & Jill Health, a Canadian telehealth company built around two condition-specific offerings: Jack for men's health and Jill for women's health. This report covers the engineering work behind turning a passive patient questionnaire into a system that scores real clinical instruments and returns an interpreted health picture back to the patient, along with the technical and clinical-data challenges that came with building it. What I want a reader to take away is that building software for healthcare means the code is only half the problem, the other half is making sure what the code produces is clinically accurate, safe, and something a patient can actually trust.



Jack & Jill Health is a Canadian digital health company operating in the telehealth space, structured around two focused offerings: Jack for men's health (conditions like hair loss and ED) and Jill for women's health (conditions like menopause and perimenopause). The company sits at the intersection of software engineering and clinical medicine, building the systems that let a small in-house medical team deliver personalized, condition-specific care to a much larger patient base than a traditional clinic could reach on its own. This split-brand structure is also what made the BioAge questionnaire work land the way it did, since the scoring engine had to branch cleanly by gender to serve both sides of the platform with clinically appropriate instruments.


Going into this term I set three main goals: getting better at managing multiple concurrent tasks without letting deadlines slip, strengthening how I collaborate on shared codebases, and pushing my technical skills (AI-driven automation, MongoDB, product analytics) past what coursework alone had covered.


On time management, keeping a disciplined ticket-tracking habit in ClickUp, breaking bigger assignments into subtasks with target dates, turned out to matter more than I expected once I was juggling bug fixes and a multi-week feature at the same time. By the end of the term I could give an accurate read on my own workload without having to think hard about it, which wasn't true in June.


Teamwork sharpened mostly through unplanned moments rather than the ones I'd scripted in my action plan. Coordinating a four-day-deadline release between the marketing team and technical leads taught me more about proactive communication than any of the code reviews did, mainly because there was no time to fix a misunderstanding after the fact.


Technically, this is where the term exceeded what I'd planned for. I went in wanting exposure to AI-driven automation, MongoDB, and product analytics; I came out having used all three to ship a clinical-grade feature, which is a different kind of proficiency than just having touched the tools.


My role centered on two projects that, together, capture most of what the term was about, and both demanded real depth in the codebase itself, not just surface-level changes.


The larger of the two was rebuilding the patient health questionnaire system from a homegrown scoring scheme into one based on nine validated clinical instruments (covering sleep, stress, mood, cognition, and several other health domains). The task arrived looking like a routine content update; it turned into a full-stack rebuild touching the questionnaire flow, a from-scratch scoring engine, and a new results page that, for the first time, actually showed patients an interpretation of their own answers. Configuring the scoring logic itself was substantial engineering work: translating validated clinical formulas into code that handled edge cases correctly, branched appropriately by instrument, and integrated cleanly with the rest of the codebase.


Alongside the coding itself, the harder problem was that the written scoring specification I was handed conflicted with the live product in several places, question wording that no longer existed, answer options that had changed, item numbers that didn't line up. Finding and resolving those mismatches before writing a line of scoring logic mattered as much as the logic itself, because a scoring engine built against the wrong spec would have quietly returned wrong results. I also had to make a judgment call partway through: after finding a display inconsistency between two related metrics, I built a fix, then reverted it once I realized a teammate's feature downstream depended on the original, untransformed values. Keeping the two consistent across the product turned out to matter more than making my own screen look cleaner.


By the end of the term, the questionnaire scoring engine was fully built, tested, and verified by hand against real data. More importantly, it's already become the foundation other projects are building on top of, work that will push Jack & Jill Health closer to actually guiding patients toward a solution for their health concerns, rather than just collecting answers from them.


If I had to summarize the term in one line, it would be that the hardest and most useful problems weren't purely algorithmic, but the coding itself is what made solving them possible in the first place. Reconciling conflicting information, communicating across teams under a deadline, and knowing when to leave a good-enough decision alone all depended on being able to actually build and understand the systems underneath them. Going in, I expected the technical goals to be the ones I'd remember. Looking back, they're still central, just alongside the judgment calls I didn't expect to learn as much from.


I'd like to thank my supervisor and the tech team at Jack & Jill Health for their guidance throughout the term, with particular thanks to Soojin, whose code reviews and feedback consistently made my work better. Thanks as well to Katie, my co-op coordinator, for her support navigating the co-op process. I'm looking forward to continuing to work with Jack & Jill Health for my next co-op term this fall!
